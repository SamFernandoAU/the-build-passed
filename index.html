The Build Passed. The Hackers Don't Care.

Fifteen comfortable lies engineering teams tell themselves about security, why they don't hold up, and what to do instead.

A talk by Sam Fernando
Written up for everyone who missed it on the day

Read the myths Get the GovernanceLite framework

// it starts on a friday

We've all been here

Friday, 4:47 PM. Thirteen minutes until the weekend. Someone on the platform team needs a new partner route on the API gateway. The gateway is all Terraform, so it's four lines in routes.tf.

A
#platform · 4:47 PM
tiny config change to the gateway for the new partner route, should be quick
# gateway/routes.tf
+ resource "gateway_route" "partner_v2" {
+   host = "api.example.com"
+   path = "/partners/v2/*"
+ }

The phrase "tiny config change" has an undefeated record. Lint passes at 4:51. All 412 tests pass. Static analysis (SAST) finds nothing high. The reviewer writes "LGTM. Ship it." and it's in production at 4:58.

Lint4:51
Tests412 passed
SAST0 high
Reviewapproved
Deploy4:58 PM

Every check is green. Everyone goes home. We'll come back to this change throughout, because it runs through most of what follows. First, let's talk about why it felt fine.

// myth 1 of 15

The build passed, so we're good.

Why we believe it

  • The pipeline is our safety net
  • Green has meant "safe to ship" for years

What's actually true

  • Tests check what we thought to test
  • Nothing checks what a change exposes

Nothing in a typical pipeline checks what a change exposes once it's live. Friday's change passed every check, and that's exactly the problem.

What green doesn't cover

Identity

Who and what can log in

Secrets

Credentials that outlive their owners

Dependencies

Code you didn't write

Infrastructure

What's exposed, and to whom

Assumptions

"That was switched off"

Every remaining myth lives in one of these five. None of them shows up as a failing unit test.

// the fix

Put security in the pipeline

A security check at every stage, as close to the developer as possible.

LaptopSecret scanning
Pull requestSAST, dependency (SCA) and IaC scanning
BuildImage scanning, SBOM
DeployPolicy checks
RuntimePosture management (CSPM), logs

Every check should be four things:

earlyautomatedactionableowned

Early, so it's cheap to fix. Automated, so it always runs. Actionable: a fix, not a lecture. Owned: a check with no owner is just noise.

// myth 2 of 15

The scanner says we're clean.

If the build only checks what we told it to, surely the scanner covers the rest? Your scanner is probably telling the truth, about the things it looks at.

SAST is good at

  • Injection
  • Hardcoded credentials
  • Dangerous functions

SAST is not good at

  • Business logic and authorisation
  • Configuration
  • "Should this endpoint exist?"

Broken Access Control is number one on the OWASP Top 10, and it's exactly the kind of flaw static analysis usually misses. Clean on what it checks. Silent on what it doesn't.

The other lie: 4,000 warnings

There's an opposite lie: the scanner that's never clean. Thousands of open findings, hundreds marked critical, none triaged. Muted channels, auto-closed tickets, "known issue". Nobody reads any of it, so the one that matters sits quietly at the bottom of the pile. A scanner with 4,000 warnings teaches one skill: ignoring scanners.

Start with what's known-exploited and internet-facing. Triage by real risk, not by severity score: CVSS tells you how bad something would be, not how likely it is to happen to you, and only a small fraction of published vulnerabilities are ever exploited. Ask five questions:

CheckHow
Known exploited?Check CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) list
Likely to be exploited?Check its EPSS score
Reachable?Is the vulnerable code actually called?
Exposed?Is it internet-facing?
Owned?Does it have a team and a date?

Filter like this and 4,000 findings usually becomes a few dozen. Suppressing a finding is fine, with a reason, an owner and an expiry date.

Dependencies are your attack surface

Your scanner checks your code. Most of your code isn't yours. The OWASP Top 10:2025 now includes Software Supply Chain Failures as its own category. Prioritise dependency findings the same way: known exploited, reachable in your code, internet-facing. The goal isn't zero findings. It's making upgrades small, frequent and boring.

The container is not a force field

Scan and rebuild

An image built in March carries March's vulnerabilities. Use small base images, rebuilt on a schedule.

Don't run as root

Drop capabilities. Use a read-only filesystem.

Pin, SBOM, sign

Pin by digest, generate an SBOM and sign what you ship, so you know exactly what's running.

FROM ubuntu:latest is not a version strategy.

The pipeline is the target

Your scanners run in your pipeline. So do your best credentials. Attackers increasingly go after what you install rather than what you write: build systems, CI actions, package registries and editor extensions. A moved tag, a malicious package version or an extension update can run inside your build with access to its secrets. Attackers don't break your code. They poison what you install.

// the fix

Harden your supply chain

Most of this is a one-line change.

  • Pin actions to a full commit SHA, not a tag. A tag can be moved. A SHA can't.
  • Read-only tokens by default, and OIDC to the cloud instead of stored keys.
  • Run SCA in every pull request, and let Dependabot or Renovate open small, frequent update PRs. Triage them the same way: known-exploited, reachable, internet-facing.
  • Don't trust brand-new versions. Install with --ignore-scripts, and upgrade often but not on day one, because malicious versions are usually caught within days.
  • Allow-list IDE and browser extensions, because a trusted extension can turn malicious in an automatic update.
  • Keep an SBOM, so "are we affected?" takes minutes, not days.

// myth 3 of 15

It's fine, it's a private repo.

Scanners look at code. But some of the most dangerous things in a repo aren't code at all. Private is a permission setting, not a guarantee.

Git is a time machine for mistakes

Deleting a secret doesn't delete history. One git log -S and there's the commit, with the live key in it:

$ git log --all -S "API_KEY" --oneline
a1b9c3f  remove api key (oops)
7e2d410  temp: add api key for testing

$ git show 7e2d410 | grep KEY
+API_KEY=live_8f3c…

Forks, clones and caches keep copies. Someone forks it to a personal account. A laptop gets an infostealer. A CI token leaks. If it was ever pushed, assume it was copied. And modern supply-chain malware doesn't need your repo at all: it reads the tokens already sitting on developer machines and build runners.

Rotate first, tidy later

When you find a live key in Git history, a revoked secret is useless to whoever copied it, and rewriting history doesn't un-copy anything.

  1. Revoke and rotate. A revoked secret is useless.
  2. Check usage. If it was valid for two years, you need to know whether anyone used it.
  3. Then clean up: history, forks, caches.

Cleaning history without rotating is just tidying the crime scene.

// the fix

Make secrets hard to leak

The best secret is one that doesn't exist.

Pre-commit scanning

Catch it before it leaves the laptop.

Push protection

Turn it on at the platform level, to catch what gets past the laptop.

A secrets manager

Not .env files in source or shared in team chats.

Workload identity

Your pipeline gets a short-lived token instead of holding a deploy key forever.

Scan Slack, Jira and Confluence too. Plenty of leaks start outside code.

PreviousMyth 2: The scanner is clean NextMyth 4: Popular packages are safe

// myth 4 of 15

Popular packages and extensions are safe.

Popularity is exactly what attackers want. Compromise one widely used package or extension and you reach everyone who installed it, often through an automatic update.

Download counts, star ratings and even "Verified" or "Featured" badges tell you something is popular, not that this week's version is safe.

// the fix

Trust versions, not names

Pin versions

Lock files and exact versions, so nothing changes without a pull request.

Add a cooldown

Wait a few days before adopting new releases. Most malicious versions are caught quickly.

Allow-list extensions

Browser and IDE extensions come from an approved list, managed centrally.

PreviousMyth 3: It's a private repo NextMyth 5: The AI wrote it

// myth 5 of 15

The AI wrote it, so it's probably fine.

AI coding tools are brilliant, and plenty of us use them every day. But treat your agent as a very fast junior developer: confident, quick, and happy to paste a secret into a config file. It doesn't know your threat model, it will happily suggest a package that doesn't exist, and it can be talked into things by text it reads along the way.

Code an agent wrote is still code your team ships. It deserves exactly the same scrutiny as code a person wrote, and the agent itself deserves the same least-privilege treatment as any other identity.

// the fix

Same checks, least-privilege agents, and a human merges

Same checks as any PR

Secret scanning, SAST, dependency checks and tests run on agent-written changes too.

Least-privilege agents

Each agent gets its own identity and short-lived credentials, scoped to what it actually needs.

A human merges

A person reviews and merges. The agent proposes; it doesn't approve its own work.

Approved tools

Agree which assistants, models and MCP servers are allowed, and what data can go into them.

PreviousMyth 4: Popular packages are safe NextMyth 6: Security slows us down

// myth 6 of 15

Security slows us down.

Usually followed by: "so we'll add it later".

Why we believe it

  • A 40-minute scan blocks the merge
  • 4,000 findings nobody can triage
  • Security says no at the end

What's actually true

  • Badly placed security is slow
  • Small, frequent changes are easier to secure
  • The slowest security happens after release

We've all lived the first column. But that's badly placed security, and yes, it is slow. Fast teams ship small changes, and small changes are easier to check, easier to fix and easier to roll back. The slowest security there is happens after release: rework, incidents, and rotating keys at 2 AM. The problem isn't security. It's where you put it. Security built into every stage of delivery lowers the cost of incidents, and teams that ship small changes often also recover faster, so their window of exposure is smaller. Skipping security doesn't save time. It moves the cost somewhere more expensive.

// the fix

Make the secure path the fast path

Budget it

Security checks get a time budget measured in minutes, like any other CI step.

In the pull request

Findings where the developer has the context, not in a report three weeks later.

Block what's exploitable

Known-exploited, reachable and exposed. Everything else is a ticket, not a gate.

Pave the road

Secure templates and defaults, so the easy way is already the safe way and nobody has to ask.

Treat a slow or noisy security check as a bug, with an owner.

PreviousMyth 5: The AI wrote it NextMyth 7: The cloud handles it

// myth 7 of 15

The cloud provider handles security.

Friday's gateway is a managed cloud service. Surely the provider has our back? Some of us have read the shared responsibility model. Some of us accepted it without reading it.

They secure the cloud. You secure what's in it.

The provider

  • Data centres and hardware
  • The hypervisor and network
  • The managed service itself

You

  • Who can log in, and whether MFA is on
  • How it's configured
  • Your data, keys and secrets
  • What you connect to it

The line moves depending on the service: a virtual machine leaves you more to do than a SaaS app. But identity, configuration and data are always yours. Friday's gateway is a managed service, and its routes are still ours. Their half is excellent. Your half is still yours.

Misconfigurations are just bugs with better networking

Security Misconfiguration sits near the top of the OWASP Top 10:2025. The classic shapes: a console login without MFA, a data platform reachable from anywhere, and old storage left behind after a migration. Migration isn't finished until the old thing is gone.

// the fix

Own your half

Four things, all in tools you already have.

MFA, enforced

At the tenant level, for every cloud and SaaS console, so no account can opt out.

Org-wide guardrails

AWS service control policies, Azure Policy, GCP organisation policies. Deny public storage by policy, not by hope.

Posture management

CSPM against the CIS Benchmarks, with findings routed to the owner.

Network allow-lists

Data platforms only reachable from where you work.

And read the shared responsibility matrix for every service you use.

PreviousMyth 6: Security slows us down NextMyth 8: MFA covers us

// myth 8 of 15

MFA means we're covered.

MFA is one of the best controls you can have. Keep it. But it protects the login, not what happens after the login.

Steal a session cookie or an OAuth token and you skip the login entirely: no password, no MFA prompt. And older MFA methods like SMS codes and push approvals can be phished or fatigued.

// the fix

Protect the session, not just the login

Phishing-resistant MFA

Passkeys or FIDO2 security keys, especially for admins.

Shorter sessions

Shorter session and token lifetimes, and re-authentication for sensitive actions.

Review OAuth grants

Regularly list the apps with access to your organisation, and remove what nobody can explain.

PreviousMyth 7: The cloud handles it NextMyth 9: It's behind the VPN

// myth 9 of 15

It's behind the VPN, so it's internal.

A VPN is just another login on the internet. Once someone is through it, "internal" services often trust them completely, with no further checks.

Old VPN profiles, shared accounts and single-factor logins are exactly the forgotten doors attackers look for.

// the fix

Authenticate every service as if it were public

Verify every request

Each service checks who is calling and whether they're allowed, not just which network they came from. That's zero trust, in plain English.

MFA on remote access

No single-factor VPN or remote-desktop logins, ever.

Retire old profiles

Remove VPN profiles, accounts and portals nobody uses any more.

PreviousMyth 8: MFA covers us NextMyth 10: Nobody knows it exists

// myth 10 of 15

Nobody knows that endpoint exists.

Back to Friday. What nobody noticed was that the apply also brought back legacy-api, an old route that had been removed by hand in the cloud portal but never from the Terraform. Everyone believed it was switched off. Security through obscurity: who's going to find it?

The internet is very good at finding things

Certificate logs

Every TLS certificate is public

DNS

Forgotten subdomains

Cloud endpoints

Public IPs, gateways

Old environments

staging2, test-old

The moment Friday's change went live, the gateway issued a certificate for legacy-api. Certificate transparency logs are public by design, and scanners watch them. Obscure lasts minutes, not years. Attackers don't need your CMDB.

Forgotten doors aren't only endpoints

Forgotten accounts and integrations are endpoints with a login screen. Run this review on your own environment:

Account or integrationWhat the review findsRisk
admin@companyShared by six peopleHIGH
contractor.daveContract ended 18 months agoHIGH
"Sales chat" OAuth appFull CRM access; nobody remembers approving itHIGH

The last row is the one most teams have never checked. An integration's token is the app's own access: no login and no MFA prompt. Forgotten, but still live, and one password or one token is enough.

If it still answers requests, it isn't retired.

// the fix

Identity without a six-month project

Most of this is settings in tools you already pay for.

Individual accounts, strong MFA

Passkeys or FIDO2. No shared admin.

Workload identity

Short-lived tokens, not stored keys.

Offboarding on the end date

Apps, integrations and logins, not when someone remembers.

Review OAuth apps and extensions

Quarterly. Remove what nobody can explain.

Follow the evidence (and the money)

To find what you've got: certificates and DNS, your cloud inventory, external scanning, gateway routes and procurement's list of contracts. And the cloud bill: if you're paying for it, it exists. Then every asset gets an owner, or a decommission date.

Your finance team knows more about your attack surface than your CMDB.

PreviousMyth 9: It's behind the VPN NextMyth 11: It's only a test environment

// myth 11 of 15

It's only a test environment.

Why we believe it

  • It's not production
  • No real customers use it
  • It'll be torn down soon

What's actually true

  • Copies of real data end up in test
  • Credentials are often shared with production
  • Temporary environments tend to live forever

Test, staging and dev environments feel low-stakes, but attackers don't see a difference. They're often reachable from the internet, run older versions nobody patches, have debug features switched on, and are watched by nobody.

Worse, they're often full of copies of production data and long-lived credentials that also work in production. Remember staging2 and test-old: forgotten environments are exactly the endpoints the internet finds first.

// the fix

Treat non-production as part of your attack surface

No production data

Use masked, synthetic or generated test data, never a straight copy of production.

Separate credentials

Nothing shared with production, and short-lived wherever possible.

The same front door

SSO, MFA and network restrictions, just like production.

An owner and an expiry date

Every environment has a named owner and a teardown date, and gets torn down on it.

If it's on the internet, it's production to an attacker.

PreviousMyth 10: Nobody knows it exists NextMyth 12: Too small to be a target

// myth 12 of 15

We're too small to be a target.

Most attacks are automated. The bot hitting your login page doesn't know or care how many staff you have: it tries leaked passwords, scans for known holes and moves on to whoever answers.

Small organisations are often easier targets, not harder ones, and they're frequently the stepping stone into a bigger customer or supplier.

// the fix

Start with the boring three

MFA

On email, cloud consoles, remote access and every admin account.

Patching

Internet-facing systems first, and anything known to be exploited, fast.

Backups

Offline or immutable copies, and a restore you've actually tested.

PreviousMyth 11: It's only a test environment NextMyth 13: We'll work it out

// myth 13 of 15

We'll work it out if it happens.

Friday night, someone found legacy-api. Saturday morning, someone has to answer: what did they get?

You can't investigate what you didn't log

Without request logs, you usually have to assume every record was taken. Good logs answer four questions:

Who

Which identity or key

What

Which records and actions

When

On a synced clock

From where

IP and region

Logging is a design decision, not an afterthought. Working it out later needs evidence you collected earlier. Check what your platforms actually record, too: some important audit logs only exist in premium licence tiers. Find out which logs you have before you need them.

// the fix

A one-page plan, written on a quiet day

Who decides

A named person, and a deputy.

Who to call

Your cloud provider, your insurer, legal, and ASD's 24/7 hotline: 1300 CYBER1 (1300 292 371).

Where the evidence is

Which logs, and how long they're kept.

How to shut a door

Revoke keys, block routes, disable accounts. Fast.

One page, short enough to read at 2 AM. Read it out loud twice a year.

PreviousMyth 12: Too small to be a target NextMyth 14: Clean pen test

// myth 14 of 15

We had a clean pen test last year.

So why did nobody spot legacy-api before Friday? "We had a pen test." This one is mostly true, and that's what makes it dangerous. Keep doing pen tests. But a pen test is a photo, not CCTV.

Point in time

It tested last year's code, and you've shipped hundreds of changes since.

In scope only

legacy-api wasn't in scope, because everyone thought it was switched off. The tester did a great job on what they were shown.

Time-boxed

A few days of testing. Attackers have all year.

A clean report is a good day, not a guarantee. Exploiting known vulnerabilities is one of the most common ways attackers get in, and they test you every day. Your pen tester visits once a year.

// the fix

From annual to always

Keep the annual pen test, and add four things around it.

Watch your attack surface

Continuous external scanning of everything you have on the internet, including the things you think are off.

Patch by exploitation

Known-exploited first. ASD's Essential Eight says internet-facing services within 48 hours when the vendor rates a vulnerability critical or a working exploit exists.

Test after big changes

Targeted retests, not just the anniversary.

Let people tell you

A vulnerability disclosure policy and a security.txt file.

Keep the annual pen test. Just stop treating it as the plan.

PreviousMyth 13: We'll work it out NextMyth 15: We're certified

// myth 15 of 15

We're ISO 27001 / SOC 2 certified.

The full myth is the part nobody says out loud: "…so we're secure." Both frameworks are worth having: they give structure, and they force good conversations.

A certificate is not a force field

ISO/IEC 27001

Certifies that you run a working system for managing security risk. It's not proof that every system is secure.

SOC 2

Type I: controls designed right, on one day. Type II: controls operated over a period, typically three to twelve months.

You can be certified on Thursday and breached on Saturday. The certificate can be on the wall, and the hackers still don't care.

// the fix

Certified, but what's next?

Certified is the floor. These six things keep you out of the news between audits.

Measure controls weekly

MFA coverage, patch age and backup success, on a dashboard someone actually looks at.

Patch to the threat

Known-exploited issues in days, not next quarter.

Prove you can recover

Restore something for real, every quarter. A backup you've never restored is a hope.

Test like an attacker

Attack-path testing aimed at your crown jewels, not just a compliance-scoped pen test.

Look outside the scope

Certificates cover a defined scope, and attackers don't read it.

Rehearse the bad day

Run the one-page plan as a tabletop exercise with leadership, twice a year.

Auditors check that you have controls. Attackers check that they work.

// the bigger picture

The new front doors

This is the map of where attackers increasingly come in. We've met most of them already.

Browser extensions

Trusted, even "Verified" extensions turned malicious by an update.

IDE extensions

Self-spreading editor extensions that steal developer tokens.

Malicious packages

Worms in package registries, and package names that AI assistants hallucinate.

Build and CI

Poison the pipeline and you poison everything it ships.

SaaS and OAuth tokens

Connected apps whose tokens skip the login and the MFA prompt.

People and platforms

Stolen staff logins that reach suppliers and the platforms you share data with.

They don't come through the front door. They come through your tools.

Breaches start in boring places: known holes, stolen logins and other people's software.

// the takeaway

GovernanceLite: guardrails, not gates

So what does a team actually run, day to day? GovernanceLite is a mini governance framework for engineering teams where AI writes a lot of the code. Eight practices. None needs a GRC platform, and each one produces evidence as a side effect of doing the work.

1 · Owners

Every service, data store, repo and AI agent. Friday's legacy-api had none.

2 · Identity

Humans and agents: SSO, phishing-resistant MFA, short-lived agent credentials.

3 · AI usage rules

Approved tools, models and MCP servers, what data can go in, and a human reviews the output.

4 · Guardrailed pipeline

Protected main, automated checks, and a human merges.

5 · Suppliers

Including AI vendors: a list, a yearly review, data-use terms checked.

6 · Logs

Including what agents do.

7 · Incident plan

One page, checked twice a year.

8 · Monthly review

30 minutes: risks, metrics, actions.

What to do next

You don't need to fix everything tomorrow. Five things, in the order I'd do them:

  1. Give everything a named owner. This one unlocks almost everything else.
  2. Remove shared and stale access, and turn on MFA everywhere.
  3. Scan for secrets, and rotate anything exposed.
  4. Put prioritised checks in CI/CD.
  5. Make sure you can answer "what was accessed?"

Pick one. Tell someone on your team. Peer pressure is an underrated security control.

Security isn't a tool you buy or a checkbox you tick. It's how your engineering team works every single day.

The build can be green, the scan can be clean, and the certificate can be framed on the wall.

The hackers still don't care.
